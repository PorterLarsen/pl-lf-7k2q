Summary

This list is owners who are behind on property taxes: people and companies under some financial or practical pressure. Investors profit from lists like this in five main ways: wholesaling, fixing and reselling, buying to rent, buying on terms (seller financing or taking over the existing loan), and bidding at the county's May tax sale.

The common thread is buying below what a retail buyer would pay. The discount comes from what you offer in exchange: speed, cash, no repairs, no showings, no commission, and the back taxes handled. A deal is fair when the owner ends up better off than their realistic alternative, which for many is losing the property at tax sale or foreclosure, or a slow, expensive retail sale.

For most of these owners the first step is the same: a respectful letter to the tax-bill address, then a conversation about what they actually need.

Vacant land, like the Hyrum and northeast Logan lots on the list, often trades at 25–50% of value from tired owners and resells to builders or neighbors. Most flip and wholesale buyers cap their price at 70% of after-repair value minus repairs; the app's target offer uses the same rule.

Do people assume the loan?

Sometimes, but less often than investor marketing suggests, and there are two very different ways.

Formal assumption (with the lender's approval). Most FHA, VA and USDA loans are assumable. You apply with the owner's servicer and qualify on credit, income and debt-to-income. With FHA you must live in the home; FHA loans do not allow assumptions for investment properties. You also pay the owner's equity in cash or with a second loan. Conventional loans are usually not assumable.

Subject-to (without asking the lender). The owner deeds you the house and you keep making their payments in their name. It's fast and needs little cash, but federal law (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3) lets lenders call the whole loan due on a transfer. The exceptions cover family transfers, divorce, inheritance and living trusts, not sales to investors. Lenders rarely call loans that are being paid, but they can, and the owner's credit rides on your payments.

A tax-delinquent owner is often behind on the mortgage too, so check the loan status first. Have a Utah real estate attorney draft any subject-to deal, and use a loan servicing company so the owner can see every payment.

Wholesaling in Utah

It's the most common way to start because it needs little cash, but in Utah it carries real licensing risk.

  1. Find a motivated owner and agree on a price well under value, for example 60–70% of after-repair value minus repairs.
  2. Sign a purchase contract naming the buyer as "you and/or assigns", with an inspection period that lets you exit.
  3. Find a cash investor who will pay more, then assign the contract for a fee, or buy and resell the same day (a double close).
  4. The investor closes with the owner at a title company and your fee is paid at closing.

The licensing problem. The Utah Division of Real Estate says anyone who offers or attempts to offer the purchase, sale, lease, management, or exchange of another person's property with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration must first obtain a Utah real estate license, and advertising counts as an offer. Marketing a house you don't own can look exactly like that.

  • Double close (actually buy, then resell) rather than advertise someone else's house.
  • Market only to a few investors you know, as an assignment of your contract.
  • Or get licensed: 120 hours of prelicensing education in Utah.
  • Tell the owner in writing that you may assign or resell for a profit.

Buying one to live in

Often the best first deal: owner-occupant loans need less down and cost less than investor loans. Buy directly from the owner, not at the tax sale, because a tax deed makes a mortgage and title insurance hard to get.

Financing

  • FHA: 3.5% down with a 580 credit score, on 1–4 unit properties. Live in one unit of a duplex or 4-plex and rent the others ("house hacking").
  • FHA 203(k) or a conventional renovation loan rolls repairs into the mortgage.
  • Assuming the owner's FHA or VA loan can keep a low rate if you qualify and cover their equity.
  • Owner-occupant loans usually require moving in within 60 days and living there at least a year.

Before you commit

  • Title search: mortgages, judgments, tax liens, HOA dues, mechanic's liens, all paid at or before closing.
  • Back property taxes paid from the sale proceeds at closing.
  • Full inspection, plus sewer scope, radon and roof on older homes.
  • Repair bids from a contractor, not the app's estimate.
  • Who lives there now. Tenants keep their lease rights after a sale, and eviction takes weeks or months.
  • Zoning and permits, especially for a basement apartment or addition.
  • Insurance quote; vacant or rough homes cost more to insure.
  • Commute, schools and the street at night.

After you move in, apply with the Cache County Assessor for the primary residential exemption, which taxes your home at 55% of its value instead of 100%.

My plan: live in, then wholesale, then rent near USU

  • Step 1, a home to live in. With about $20,000 cash, an FHA 203(k) loan is the usual route for a fixer: 3.5% down, and the repair costs go into the same mortgage. You must live there at least a year. Asking the seller to pay closing costs stretches your cash much further. Set Settings → My plan to “A home to live in” and turn on the Fits my cash filter. Each card then shows your cash to close and an estimated monthly payment. Get pre-approved first: your income sets the loan size.
  • House hack. A 2–4 unit property with FHA lets you live in one unit and rent the others, so the tenants help pay the mortgage. That folds your rental goal into step 1. Look at multi-family leads close to USU.
  • Step 2, wholesaling. Without a license, contract a property you actually intend to buy, and either close on it yourself or assign the contract with full disclosure. Don't market properties you don't control. Switch the plan to “Wholesaling”.
  • Step 3, rentals. Student demand is strongest within about 2 miles of USU. The “Rentals” plan favors multi-family and distance to USU. Use the Within X miles of USU filter and the Mi to USU column.
  • Is a Utah license easy? It's moderate. You need 120 hours of approved pre-licensing coursework, then the state and national exam (Pearson VUE). You must apply within 90 days of passing and work under a principal broker. Expect about 3–6 months and roughly $500–$1,300 in total. After that it's 18 hours of continuing education every 2 years. It lets you market deals legally, earn commission on your own purchases, and get MLS access. Most buyers working toward their own homes and rentals skip it at first.

  • Stack them. A 203(k) or HomeStyle loan on a duplex near USU, plus UHC assistance and seller-paid closing costs, can get you in for only a few thousand dollars and covers steps 1 and 3 at once.
  • Live-in flip. After 2 years in a home you lived in, up to $250,000 of profit ($500,000 married) is tax-free when you sell. Then buy the next one with a new owner-occupied loan.
  • Keep it as a rental. After a year you can move out, rent the first place, and buy the next home with another low-down owner-occupied loan.
  • Tax deed sale (cash). The county auctions the eligible parcels each May. Bids are cash, and the title often needs a quiet-title action before a lender will lend on it.

Treating sellers fairly and legal guardrails

  • Foreclosure rescue rules. Utah treats "foreclosure rescue" done for compensation or with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration as licensed real estate activity. With an owner facing foreclosure, don't promise to "save" the home, and never use a leaseback or buyback promise an attorney hasn't structured.
  • Show the owner the math. The app's comparison to listing explains your price honestly.
  • Give them time. Encourage owners to talk with family, an agent or an attorney before signing.
  • Disclose your intent to resell or assign, in writing.
  • Calls and texts. Check the National Do Not Call Registry first and stop when asked. Letters are the safest first contact.
  • Tax sale protections. Owners can protest a sale within a deadline after it, and leftover money from the sale belongs to the former owner.

Sync between devices

Keeps your statuses, notes, follow-ups, letters and settings the same on your phone and computer. Everything is encrypted with a passphrase you choose before it leaves the device, then stored in a private file (a "secret gist") in your own GitHub account. Without the passphrase it's unreadable, even to GitHub.

One-time setup: create a GitHub token
  1. On GitHub, go to Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens → Fine-grained tokens → Generate new token (or open github.com/settings/personal-access-tokens/new).
  2. Name it Lead Finder sync and set an expiration (1 year is fine).
  3. Under Permissions → Account permissions, set Gists to Read and write. Leave everything else as is.
  4. Tap Generate token and copy it. It can only read and write your gists, nothing else.

If you forget the passphrase, the synced copy can't be opened. Your data on each device stays put, and you can start over with a new passphrase.

Skip tracing: owner phone numbers & emails

A skip tracing service matches each owner to their phone numbers and emails, for roughly 7–15¢ per owner found. No subscription needed.

  1. On the Leads tab, filter to the leads worth calling (for example Score 70+). The file uses your current filters and skips passed leads, leads already traced, and duplicates.
  2. Tap Download skip trace file below.
  3. Upload it to a pay-per-match service such as BatchData's pay-as-you-go skip tracing (batchdata.io), REISkip or similar. The columns are already named the way they expect. Turn on their Do Not Call / litigator scrub if offered.
  4. Download their results file and tap Import results. Phones and emails attach to each lead, with mobile/landline and Do Not Call flags.

Calling rules: check numbers against the National Do Not Call Registry (most services flag them), dial by hand, and don't send mass or automated texts to cell phones. Federal law (the TCPA) restricts those without consent. Letters have no such limits.

Where the data comes from

Unpaid back taxes: Cache County Treasurer's public delinquent-tax map service. Lot size, building size, year built and market value: Utah's statewide tax-parcel layer (UGRC), updated yearly by the county assessor. Tax-sale eligible means taxes have gone unpaid long enough that the county can list the parcel at its May tax sale unless it's paid first. Market value is the assessor's figure, not an appraisal. Scores are for sorting only: check title, liens and condition before making an offer.
